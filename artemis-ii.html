Artemis II: Humanity's Return Beyond Earth Orbit

The real-world mission that feels closest to Project Hail Mary — deep-space isolation, cutting-edge spacecraft, and human survival beyond the safety of low Earth orbit.

What Is Artemis II?

Artemis II is the first crewed mission of NASA's Artemis program and the first human voyage beyond low Earth orbit since Apollo 17 in 1972. Scheduled to launch as early as September 2025, this mission will carry four astronauts aboard the Orion spacecraft on a ten-day flight around the Moon and back.

The mission serves as a critical proving ground for the systems, procedures, and endurance required for future lunar surface landings and eventually Mars. Unlike Artemis I, which sent an uncrewed Orion around the Moon in 2022, Artemis II puts human lives at the center of every system test.

For the official mission overview, visit NASA's Artemis II mission page and the main Artemis program portal.

Key Mission Details

  • Crew: Commander Reid Wiseman, Pilot Victor Glover, Mission Specialist Christina Koch, and Mission Specialist Jeremy Hansen — representing NASA and the Canadian Space Agency.
  • Spacecraft: The Orion crew module is designed for deep-space travel, equipped with advanced life support, radiation shielding, and autonomous navigation capabilities. (Official Orion page)
  • Launch Vehicle: The Space Launch System (SLS) is the most powerful rocket ever built, producing 8.8 million pounds of thrust at liftoff — enough to send Orion beyond the Moon. (Official SLS page)
  • Trajectory: A free-return trajectory that loops around the far side of the Moon, taking the crew farther from Earth than any humans have traveled before.
  • Duration: Approximately 10 days from launch to splashdown, with critical system checks conducted throughout every phase.

Artemis II is more than a technology demonstration. It is a statement of intent: humanity is returning to deep space, and this time we plan to stay.

Real Science vs Fiction

Many of Project Hail Mary's most compelling technologies have real-world counterparts in development. Here is how today's space science stacks up against Andy Weir's visionary inventions.

Hybrid

Nuclear Propulsion

Project Hail Mary relies on astrophage-based propulsion, but NASA is actively developing nuclear thermal and nuclear electric propulsion for deep-space missions. Nuclear thermal rockets could cut Mars transit time to 3-4 months — far shorter than chemical propulsion. The technology is real, tested on the ground, and awaiting a flight demonstration. While we lack astrophage, nuclear propulsion is the closest real analogue to the Hail Mary's drive system.

Plausible within 20-30 years
Reality

Deep-Space Hibernation

Grace's induced coma during transit has a real counterpart: torpor-induced hibernation for astronauts. Spaceworks, a NASA partner, has demonstrated that therapeutic hypothermia could reduce metabolic rates by 50-70%, cutting life support mass for Mars missions. The technology is in early testing, and a crewed demonstration on Artemis-class missions could validate the concept before it is needed for interplanetary travel.

Likely within 10-15 years
Reality

AI Autonomy

Orion already operates with significant autonomy, and NASA's intelligent systems research aims to give spacecraft the ability to diagnose and respond to problems without ground control. Project Hail Mary pushes this further with a fully autonomous ship capable of interstellar navigation. Real-world AI is not there yet, but every Artemis mission brings NASA closer to spacecraft that can think for themselves.

Plausible as capability grows
Reality

Radiation Shielding

Artemis II will measure radiation exposure beyond low Earth orbit with accuracy never before possible. Modern shielding uses water, polyethylene, and active monitoring — but Project Hail Mary imagines a more advanced system capable of protecting Grace during years of interstellar exposure. Real radiation protection technology is advancing but remains one of the biggest hurdles for Mars and beyond.

Incremental progress expected
Speculative

Interstellar Propulsion

The Hail Mary reaches Tau Ceti in years — an impossible feat with current technology. Breakthrough Starshot proposes light-sail propulsion to reach Alpha Centauri in 20 years, but crewed interstellar travel remains firmly in the realm of fiction. Artemis II's lunar missions are the first steps on a path that may one day lead to the stars, but we are centuries, not decades, from an interstellar Hail Mary.

Not realistic with current physics

Artemis II Astronaut Profiles

Meet the four astronauts who will carry humanity back to the Moon. Their backgrounds, expertise, and mission roles echo the characters of Project Hail Mary in surprising ways.

KW
Christina Koch
NASA Astronaut — Mission Specialist

Christina Koch holds the record for the longest single spaceflight by a woman (328 days). An electrical engineer by training, she has conducted six spacewalks and contributed to research in life sciences, physics, and Earth observation. Her endurance, technical versatility, and calm under pressure make her the Artemis II crew member most like Grace — a scientist-astronaut who thrives when the mission demands the unexpected.

PHM parallel: Like Grace, Koch combines deep scientific skill with the emotional stamina to handle extended isolation and high-stakes problem solving.

View official NASA bio →

VG
Victor Glover
NASA Astronaut — Pilot

Victor Glover is a captain in the U.S. Navy, a test pilot, and a veteran of SpaceX Crew-1, where he served as the first African American crew member on the International Space Station. With degrees in engineering, military science, and flight test, Glover brings operational discipline and leadership to the Artemis II cockpit. His role echoes Yao's command presence — steady, procedural, and prepared for any contingency.

PHM parallel: Like Yao, Glover represents the disciplined commander who keeps the mission on track through rigorous procedure and calm authority.

View official NASA bio →

RW
Reid Wiseman
NASA Astronaut — Commander

Reid Wiseman is a veteran naval aviator and test pilot who spent 165 days aboard the ISS on Expedition 40/41, serving as chief of the Astronaut Office before being named Artemis II commander. His experience includes spacewalks, robotic operations, and the kind of decisive leadership that space exploration demands. Wiseman's blend of hands-on expertise and command responsibility mirrors the best of real-world astronaut tradition.

PHM parallel: While Grace is a reluctant hero thrust into command, Wiseman embodies the trained professional who steps up knowing exactly what the mission requires.

View official NASA bio →

Note: The fourth Artemis II crew member, Canadian Space Agency astronaut Jeremy Hansen, brings unique expertise in fighter pilot training and mission operations. His addition represents the international cooperation that defines modern space exploration — a real echo of the collaborative spirit Grace and Rocky embody in Project Hail Mary.
